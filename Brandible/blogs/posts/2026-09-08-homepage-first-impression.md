Someone lands on your homepage. They didn't call you, they didn't get a referral yet, they're just checking. They want to know if you're worth a few more minutes of their time.

If the page doesn't answer that fast, they're gone.

This isn't about having a pretty site. It's about whether the right information is in the right place, in plain language, before a visitor loses patience.

## The Three Questions Every Homepage Has to Answer

There are three things a visitor needs to know within the first few seconds. If any of them are missing or buried, the page is working against you.

**What do you do?**

Not your company name. Not a tagline about being "passionate about excellence." What, specifically, do you offer? Roofing. Physical therapy. Bookkeeping. Estate planning. Tax prep for freelancers.

If a stranger can't read that off your homepage in under ten seconds, it's not there clearly enough.

**Who do you help?**

"Businesses and individuals" is not an answer. The more specific you are, the more the right people feel like you're talking to them. Homeowners in Hunterdon County. Small medical practices. New Jersey restaurants. Residential landlords.

You're not narrowing your market by being specific. You're making the right people lean in.

**How do they get started?**

What's the next step? Call, book, fill out a form, get a quote? One clear action. Not five buttons pulling in different directions.

If you send a potential customer to a page with no obvious next step, they'll find a competitor who has one.

## Run This Test Right Now

Open your homepage on your phone. Don't scroll. Don't click anything.

Can you answer all three questions from what's visible before you scroll?

What you do, who you help, how to get started. If the answer to any of those is "you'd have to keep reading," that's the problem.

The phone test matters because a lot of visitors arrive on a screen smaller than your hand. If your headline is cropped, your phone number requires scrolling, or your navigation stacks into a broken hamburger menu, the impression you're making is not the one you think.

Now do the same test on a laptop. Same questions. Same standard.

## What's Usually Wrong

**The headline is about you, not them.**

"Welcome to Johnson Brothers Contracting" tells me nothing. "Licensed Roofing and Siding for Central NJ Homeowners" tells me everything I need to know in one line. Swap your welcome headline for a line that says what you do and for whom.

**The call to action is too shy.**

A link buried in a paragraph isn't a CTA. Neither is a "learn more" button that goes to your about page. Your primary action, call, book, quote, whatever it is, should be visible above the scroll on both mobile and desktop. Make it a button. Use plain language: "Get a Free Quote," "Book a Consultation," "Call Now."

**The phone number isn't clickable.**

If your number is in a graphic or written as plain text that doesn't open the dialer, you've just created friction for someone who is ready to call. On mobile, every phone number should be a tap-to-call link. This one takes five minutes to fix.

**The above-the-fold section tries to do too much.**

Headline, subheadline, two different CTAs, a background video, rotating testimonials, and a badge for every certification you hold. Visitors don't read crowded pages. They scan. Pick the most important thing and let it breathe.

**There's no evidence anyone has worked with you.**

A review, a recognizable client name, a specific result, a number of jobs completed. Something that tells a first-time visitor that other people have trusted you and it went well. This doesn't need to be elaborate. Even a single quoted review with a name and a city does real work.

## What a Working Homepage Actually Looks Like

You land on it. In a few seconds you know: this company does residential HVAC in Central New Jersey, they've been doing it since 2011, you can book online or call the number right at the top, and there are a dozen reviews that all say the same thing about showing up on time.

You don't have to dig for any of it. You already know enough to pick up the phone.

That's the standard. Clear, specific, and easy to act on. Not beautiful in a design-award way. Useful in a "I'm calling this person" way.

## When a Patch Won't Fix It

Sometimes the problem isn't copy. It's structure.

If your homepage was built in a template that puts the menu in the wrong place, buries the phone number in the footer, and opens with a full-screen image that says nothing, rewriting the headline isn't going to fix it. The layout itself is working against the message.

If you've already tried fixing the words and the phone still doesn't ring, the problem might be below the surface: load time, mobile layout, a form that doesn't submit correctly, or navigation that makes it hard to find a service page.

That's when a rebuild, not a refresh, is the conversation worth having.
